It's been a week and a half since the midterms, and most of the dust has settled, so I wanted to write down some reflections before the news cycle moves on too much (damn you, Jensen...). A few races technically haven't been called yet, but I'm going to make assumptions on where they land and if I'm wrong, then so be it.

Democrats won the House with about 260 seats and the Senate with 56. For the last 10 years I've been waiting for the country to give Trump the kind of repudiation I thought he deserved from the start. 2020 was a narrow and underwhelming win, and after 2024 I had mostly given up on it. It finally happened, and damn, it feels really good.

Reflecting on this race, I want to cover four things: how big this win was, why it happened, what Democrats did right, and what I think they should do with the next two years.

## The Scale of the Win

Let's start with the House popular vote, since it's the closest thing a midterm has to a national referendum. In 2024, Republicans won it by 2.6 points. This year Democrats won it by about 14. That's a swing of 16.5 points in two years.

<MidtermScaleCharts />

The only midterm since World War II with a bigger swing is 2010, when the Tea Party wave hit Obama. It's the biggest swing against a Republican president in that entire stretch: bigger than Watergate, bigger than 2006, and much bigger than Trump's own first midterm in 2018.

**Democrats won the House popular vote by the widest margin either party has managed since 1974.**

Impressive as that may be, the Senate is arguably even more impressive. Republicans lost 9 seats, the most a president's party has lost since 1958 (tied with 1994 and 2014), and they lost them in Texas, Kansas, South Carolina, and Mississippi.

The seat count in the House doesn't quite reflect the scale of the popular vote win. About 45 seats is a lot, but 2010 was 63. I wrote in my [Labor Day Prediction post](/blog/labor-day-2026-midterm-prediction) about how gerrymandering has removed most of the competitive districts in the country, and that's still true: a 16-point swing used to buy you a lot more than 45 seats.

Speaking of that post: turns out I was way too cautious. On Labor Day I predicted 51 Senate seats and 230 House seats. By late October I had moved to 53 and 236 (mostly due to the Siena polling that came out shortly after my initial prediction). I never got around to posting that one, so I guess you'll just have to trust me. But it doesn't matter because both were well short. The funny part is that the one method that I thought had yielded an 'impossible' result actually was the most accurate. The keys model said Republicans would lose 40 to 60 House seats, and it actually happened.

The polls missed too, just in the direction nobody was worried about. The generic ballot average on [the tracker](https://elections.ajaycent.com) finished at D+8.8, and the actual result was about 5 points better than that. In my [post on polling error](/blog/how-wrong-can-the-polls-be) I was mostly bracing for a miss toward Republicans, like 2016 and 2020. And the Ohio and Texas disagreement between the polls and the markets got settled: Brown and Talarico both won, so the markets' red-state discount turned out to be superstition.

## Trump's Unpopularity

So why did this happen? It wasn't because Democrats cracked the code, **this was mostly a referendum on a historically unpopular president.**

<ApprovalMidtermCharts />

Trump went into Election Day at about 38% approval on the tracker's average, and several individual polls had him in the low 30s. That's Bush-in-2006 territory, and the only president since World War II who was clearly lower going into a midterm was Truman in 1946. Presidents down there tend to lose a lot of seats, so it's not that unexpected.

The unique part is that almost none of this was bad luck. Let's look at how he got there.

<ApprovalTimelineChart />

His approval was 46% in May of last year and it fell almost in a straight line, and every step down has his name on it.

**The tariffs** were a tax on everything that he chose to impose, that the Supreme Court ruled illegal in February, and that he then brought back under a different law within hours.

**The Iran war** was a war nobody asked for and Congress never authorized. Gas was $2.98 the day it started and was over $4.30 by October. He drained the Strategic Petroleum Reserve to hide the price and the price went up anyway. (read more about this in [my post about oil prices and the SPR](/blog/state-of-the-iran-war))

**The ICE policy** was sold as going after "the worst of the worst" and turned into masked agents in American cities and two people killed on camera in Minneapolis. In the exit polls, 57% of voters said his immigration enforcement had gone too far. This was supposed to be his (and Republicans') best issue.

Add in the healthcare subsidies he let expire, and you get an electorate where the economy was the top issue and almost 9 in 10 voters said they were worried about the price of groceries and gas. He ran on bringing prices down, then spent two years on things that pushed them up. These were all own goals.

## The Big Tent

Trump being unpopular doesn't automatically mean Democrats win (at least not at this scale). So I want to give Democrats some credit here, because they did something they haven't done in a decade: they let different candidates run different races in different places.

For most of the last 10 years, the party acted like every candidate had to pass the same checklist, and anyone who didn't was treated as a problem. This year they dropped the purity politics. In Michigan they ran Abdul El-Sayed, a Medicare for All progressive. In North Carolina they ran Roy Cooper, a moderate former governor. In Ohio it was Sherrod Brown and his labor populism, in Alaska it was Mary Peltola, and all of them won.

And then there's Texas.

<SenateTrumpStateChart />

James Talarico is a former public school teacher and a seminarian who talks about his faith more comfortably than most Republicans do. He beat Jasmine Crockett in the primary, and then he beat Ken Paxton by 12 points in a state Trump won by almost 14. The other red-state flips, like Iowa, Ohio, Kansas, and South Carolina, were all 4 or 5 point wins. Talarico more than doubled them, and he's the first Democrat to win a Senate race in Texas since 1988.

**A party that has room for both El-Sayed and Talarico is a party that can win in 56 Senate seats' worth of states.** I hope they remember that in 2028.

## The Next Two Years

Now for the fun part: what can we look forward to with Democrats in control?

Let's be realistic first. Trump is still president and he still has a veto. Overriding one takes 290 votes in the House and 67 in the Senate, and Democrats have about 260 and 56. So nothing becomes law unless he signs it or about a dozen Republican senators break with him. What Democrats control now is the agenda: what gets voted on, what gets investigated, and what goes into the bills that have to pass, like the budget and the debt limit next spring.

I think they should run two agendas at the same time:

**The first is affordability.** Repeal the tariffs by taking the tariff power back for Congress, which is where the Supreme Court already said it belongs. And expand healthcare, starting with restoring the subsidies that expired this year. Pass both and make Trump veto them. Either he signs and people's costs come down, or he vetoes and every voter knows exactly who is keeping prices high. In an age of social media, the value of passing bills that you know will get vetoed has gained an optics-based value that Democrats have thus-far undervalued in my opinion.

**The second is anti-corruption.** I don't think Democrats can hold everyone accountable in two years, but they can start chipping away at the low hanging fruit. Impeach Pete Hegseth, who ran a war Congress never authorized. Impeach RFK Jr., who presided over the worst measles year since 1991 and said we weren't struggling with outbreaks (I'm personally salivating at the idea of Annie Andrews, a pediatrician, holding RFK Jr.'s feet to the fire here). I actually believe there's a reasonable possibility for some Republicans to break ranks on these. This election seems to have shaken some members out of their strict partisanship like rats fleeing a sinking ship.

I know the pushback to this: voters will just see it as lawfare, and Democrats will look like they care more about revenge than about prices. I don't buy it. Going after your enemies for things they didn't do is lawfare, which we've seen from the Trump regime so far. But choosing NOT to go after people who actually broke the law, because it's politically inconvenient, is ALSO lawfare. It tells everyone that the law only applies if you lose.

And this is where I think things are different from the last time Democrats had power. Biden was a lot of things, but he wasn't exactly an effective communicator (certainly not in his advanced age), and the case for accountability mostly never got made. Now the party has superstars like Jon Ossoff and Pete Buttigieg, who can explain to a normal person why corruption makes their life more expensive.

As for me, I'm just excited to start writing about anything other than politics for a little while lol.
